## Emotional craft

- Design the emotional arc before the beats (valley → pivot → build → resolution); two adjacent beats with the same feeling
  means one can go.
- Write to one person in one situation ("a mother at 9 p.m., kid in pyjamas, homework not done"), not a segment.
- Specificity: "Tuesday, 6:40, the bus is in four minutes and she can't find the left shoe" beats "busy mornings".
- Show, don't tell: never name the emotion ("frustrating", "amazing"); show its evidence (the sigh, the 14 unread reminders).
- Stakes: if the hero doesn't get this, what do they lose? Raise the personal stake (pride, a relationship, an evening)
  rather than inflating the external one.
- Tension and release: hold the "but" a beat longer than feels comfortable before the turn.
- Humour comes from truth plus surprise; it relieves tension and never mocks the viewer's pain.
- Sell from feelings; features are the proof. (Long-term effectiveness research on the IPA databank found emotional
  campaigns outperform rational ones on brand and business effects.)
- **Comfort, don't frighten** (parents, health, money, grief): name the worry small and recognisable, then dissolve it within
  a beat; never stage the worst case; reframe with something true; give a step small enough to start tonight; no urgency
  tricks, shame or countdowns.
- Endings land on an image of the new normal plus one line; the emotional peak comes before the logo, not on it.

## Writing for the ear

- Say it before you write it. If you wouldn't say it, don't write it.
- Short sentences, few dependent clauses, contractions, subject-verb-object, the verb early.
- Vary length on purpose: mostly 8 to 14 words, a short line for a payoff, one longer flowing line for an image. A run of
  three or more fragments is a tic. (A cadence check: if over 30% of sentences have ≤ 6 words, or 3+ short sentences run in
  a row, merge them.)
- Concrete nouns, active verbs ("Your phone buzzes: thirsty" beats "receive smart hydration notifications").
- One idea per sentence; at most one new term per sentence; the important word at the end, where the voice lands.
- Words people say: "people" not "individuals", "help" not "facilitate".
- Rule of three only when there really are three, and once per script.
- Callbacks: plant a word or image early, pay it back changed at the end; name the planted beat in the beat sheet.
- Jargon never in the hook; elsewhere only if the viewer uses it themselves (check reviews and forums).
- Numbers: one per sentence; rounded when spoken, exact on screen; units spoken; never speak URLs, versions or codes.
- Title before name for the ear ("our nurse lead, Ana Ruiz"); repeat the noun instead of an ambiguous pronoun.
- Write the silence: mark `[beat]` after the turn and before the CTA.
- Don't narrate the picture. Mute each line in your head: if the picture already says it, cut the line.
- **Sound-on or muted is decided per destination.** Feeds (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn) are muted-first: the on-screen
  text track carries the story alone (≤ 6 words per card, in reading order, readable in its time). TikTok, YouTube and web
  players are sound-on: voice and music lead, text supports. Either way, the hook reads muted. (The widely quoted "85%
  watched without sound" traces to two publishers' 2016 Facebook numbers; don't quote it.)
- B-roll text mirrors the audio literally; clever synonyms ("HOOPS" for "basketball") cost comprehension.
- The most common missing line in ads is the **outcome**: what the viewer will see, and by when.

## AI-sounding tells

**Bans** (rewrite on one sighting):
1. Contrast reveals: "It's not X, it's Y", "Not just X, but Y". State Y with its reason.
2. Negation lists: "No X. No Y. Just Z." Say what does happen.
3. Rhetorical question then answer: "The result? …", "Why does this matter? Because…".
4. Colon reveals: "One word: speed."
5. Openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "Imagine…", "What if…", "In a world where…", "Meet X", "Introducing", "Whether
   you're a X or a Y", "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing".
6. Fake-profound kickers: "And that changes everything." "Because you deserve better." "The future of X is here."
7. Marketing stock: seamless, effortless, unlock, unleash, elevate, empower, supercharge, game-changer, revolutionary, next
   level, "say goodbye to", "transform the way you", all-in-one, "in just a few clicks".
8. AI vocabulary: delve, tapestry, testament, realm, embark, journey (figurative), landscape, robust, pivotal, intricate,
   meticulous, showcase, foster, crucial.
9. Inflated significance and weasel authority: "a pivotal moment", "experts agree", "studies show".
10. Trailing pile-ons: a complete claim followed by ", ensuring…", ", making…".
11. Em and en dashes in VO scripts and on-screen text (they're also unreliable TTS pauses; use commas or full stops).

**Caps** (once per script, and only on purpose): fragment drumbeats ("Simple. Fast. Done."), reflexive threes, false ranges
("from startups to enterprises"), synonym cycling (app, platform, tool, solution for one thing), abstract subjects ("the
platform empowers teams"), uniform rhythm, "serves as / boasts" for "is / has", rhetorical-question hooks, ticker shapes ("A
doc? A wiki? No: all of them."), escalation shapes ("Hard. Insanely hard."). Never two capped devices in one beat, and never
in both the hook and the CTA.

**Spanish equivalents**: "No es solo X, es Y", "Imagina…", "Descubre…", "Dile adiós a…", "lleva tu X al siguiente nivel",
"de forma fácil y rápida", "sin complicaciones", "potencia", "experiencia" as filler, the gerund of posteriority ("lanzó la
app, logrando…"), English word-order calques.

Tests: the **swap test** (would this line work unchanged for a competitor? rewrite it), the **specificity test** (each claim
has a number, a name, a mechanism or a concrete action), the **stacking test**. Don't de-slop by inventing: a made-up number
is worse than "industry-leading".

## Localisation

- Write natively in the target language, or transcreate from an intent column; never translate line by line. Keep an
  "intent" note per line in the master script.
- Register is decided once in the brief (tú, usted or vos; tu or vous; du or Sie), per market. In much of Latin America
  usted is common even among friends; young consumer brands often use tú; voseo dominates in Argentina and Uruguay.
- "Neutral Spanish" buys reach and costs warmth. For a one-market film, write that market's Spanish and list words to avoid
  from other markets (for a Colombian audience: Spain's "deberes, móvil", Mexico's "kínder, ahorita").
- Idioms, puns, rhymes and alliteration rarely survive; plan an equivalent.
- Romance-language versions of an English script usually run longer; re-time from the real TTS and cut words rather than
  speed up the voice.
- Say numbers, dates and money the way the market does ("cincuenta mil pesos"; "mil millones"); on screen, local separators
  and date order.
- Gendered languages: choose deliberately (generic forms, inclusive alternatives, alternating) and keep it consistent
  across VO, captions and cards.
- Brand names and loanwords need a pronunciation lexicon (the spoken layer in `formats.md`; the voice-direction skill handles pronunciation).
