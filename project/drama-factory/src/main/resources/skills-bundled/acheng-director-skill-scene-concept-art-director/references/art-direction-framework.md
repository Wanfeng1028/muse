# Art Direction Framework

Use this reference when the user wants a complete scene image concept, environment key art, or a high-level art direction brief.

## Scene Thesis

Start with a one-sentence thesis:

`A [place] where [human/cultural/natural force] collides with [opposing force], creating [emotion].`

Good scene theses imply design decisions:

- A mountain temple rebuilt as a solar observatory: sacred ritual vs scientific precision.
- A luxury hotel lobby after a monsoon blackout: polished wealth vs vulnerable weather.
- A near-future food market under orbital debris: everyday warmth vs cosmic threat.

Avoid theses that only name a style:

- Weak: "A beautiful cyberpunk street."
- Strong: "A rain-soaked repair alley where low-income mechanics keep obsolete service robots alive with ceremonial care."

## World-Class Aesthetic Standard

Use these checks before calling a design "premium" or "cinematic":

- The focal idea is readable in three seconds.
- The scene has a cultural, economic, or ritual reason to exist.
- The architecture and objects share a material logic.
- The image has foreground, midground, background, and atmospheric depth.
- Light has a motivated source and reveals the story.
- Ornament follows a system instead of appearing as random decoration.
- The design has one memorable contradiction, not five unrelated gimmicks.
- The scene includes evidence of use: wear, repair, paths, tools, residue, signage, thresholds, storage, security, offerings, weathering.

## Eastern Aesthetic Lenses

Use specific principles rather than vague "Asian" styling:

- Negative space: let emptiness hold emotional pressure.
- Borrowed scenery: frame distant mountains, water, sky, city, or moon through architecture.
- Asymmetrical balance: place the focal mass off-center and counterweight it with light, mist, shadow, or a small human figure.
- Seasonal impermanence: rain, thaw, fallen leaves, incense smoke, dust, bloom, moss, rust, melting snow.
- Threshold rituals: gates, courtyards, screens, bridges, steps, lanterns, tea, offerings, washing, shoes, bells, curtains.
- Material restraint: stone, timber, paper, bronze, lacquer, ceramic, bamboo, silk, lime plaster, aged tile.
- Atmosphere over spectacle: quiet depth, layered silhouettes, compressed emotion.

Use these lenses with geographic and historical care. A Tang palace, a Kyoto machiya, a Himalayan monastery, a Suzhou garden, and a tropical Southeast Asian shrine have different spatial logic, roofs, materials, climate, and ornament.

## Western Aesthetic Lenses

Use specific traditions and production-design logic:

- Classical order: axis, proportion, colonnade, civic monumentality, rhythm.
- Gothic verticality: pointed arches, ribbing, height, colored light, sacred scale.
- Renaissance clarity: perspective, measured geometry, human-centered order.
- Baroque drama: diagonal movement, theatrical light, strong chiaroscuro, emotional excess.
- Romantic sublime: tiny figures against weather, cliffs, ruins, storms, vast nature.
- Modernist discipline: exposed structure, honest materials, functional grids, glass, steel, concrete.
- Film noir tension: slatted shadows, wet pavement, low angles, practical lights, moral ambiguity.
- Hollywood production clarity: readable silhouettes, motivated lighting, strong staging, iconic hero object, coherent set dressing.

## Cultural Synthesis

When blending Eastern and Western references:

1. Choose a dominant spatial grammar.
2. Choose a secondary material or lighting grammar.
3. Explain why the cultures meet: trade, migration, conquest, restoration, tourism, ecology, religion, diaspora, technology, or commerce.
4. Limit motifs to two or three families.
5. Add practical details that prove the place is used by people.

Example synthesis:

- Dominant space: Suzhou garden courtyard.
- Secondary grammar: Venetian decayed plaster and canal light.
- Reason: a riverside diplomatic guesthouse built for maritime trade.
- Motifs: moon gates, water reflections, arched loggias.
- Use detail: wet slippers, folded silk umbrellas, brass room keys, tea steam near carved stone drains.

## Scene Design Recipe

For each scene, specify:

- Location: exact type of place, not just genre.
- Time: era, season, day phase, recent event.
- Function: what people do there.
- Power structure: who owns, controls, maintains, or fears it.
- Materials: primary, secondary, accent, aging behavior.
- Iconic object: one unforgettable design anchor.
- Human scale: figure, door, vehicle, stair, furniture, animal, tool, or shadow.
- Weather/atmosphere: physically plausible and story-relevant.
- Offscreen world: what the image implies beyond the frame.

## Anti-Generic Moves

If a concept feels generic:

- Replace broad genre labels with a specific profession, ritual, industry, or social class.
- Add one trace of recent action.
- Define who maintains the space and how.
- Add a local climate effect.
- Give ornament a rule, such as "all sacred geometry appears only on thresholds."
- Remove three decorative details and strengthen one hero shape.
- Add one imperfect human-scale object.
