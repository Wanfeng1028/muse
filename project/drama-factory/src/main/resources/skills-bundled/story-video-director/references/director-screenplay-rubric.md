# Director screenplay rubric

Use this before assets or prompts, especially when the user provides only a theme, idiom, moral, article, or one-line idea.

## Theme to story

Write one sentence each:

1. **Theme** — the human truth or question, without repeating the title.
2. **Dramatic question** — what the audience wants answered.
3. **Protagonist** — a specific person or creature with a recognizable habit.
4. **Objective** — what the protagonist actively tries to get now.
5. **False belief / pressure** — the belief, fear, rule, or temptation that shapes the choice.
6. **Visible choice** — the action that cannot be taken back.
7. **Consequence** — an observable change in situation, relationship, or world.
8. **Final image** — a concrete image that carries the meaning without explanation.

## Short-film arc

For a short under 60 seconds, prefer four movements:

- **Hook**: an unusual action, contradiction, or danger in the first 1–3 seconds.
- **Pattern**: establish what the protagonist repeats or believes.
- **Break**: escalate, reverse, or expose the cost of the pattern.
- **Afterimage**: hold the changed world or character long enough for the audience to interpret it.

A familiar story may change setting, object, age, season, or point of view, but preserve the causal chain and emotional truth. Avoid adding side plots that cannot pay off.

## Scene and shot test

Each shot must have one sentence in this form:

`Because [previous state], [character] tries [objective], but [obstacle], so [visible change].`

If a shot cannot complete this sentence, merge it, cut it, or redesign it. A shot may be quiet, but it must still change information, pressure, emotion, or consequence.

For acted scenes, use the scene card and beat-to-prompt method in [performance-to-prompt.md](performance-to-prompt.md). Test the **listener** as rigorously as the speaker: identify the stimulus, the listener's immediate tactic (accept, deny, bargain, hide, delay), the visible behavior, and the resulting change. A departure or death announcement with no response or carried emotion is a scene failure even when the line and portrait are attractive.

Comedy needs a specific false expectation and a consequential reversal. State whose objective produces the joke, whose reaction releases it, and where the audience has time to recognize it. Do not replace this with instructions such as “funny acting.”

## Director's visual decisions

Choose one primary visual metaphor and track its state across the film: intact/broken, full/empty, high/low, open/closed, warm/cold, cultivated/wild, near/far, or another concrete opposition. Use production design, blocking, camera distance, light, and sound to reveal the metaphor. Do not explain the metaphor in dialogue unless the user requests an instructional format.

Use one dominant camera grammar per project and break it only for a reason: locked observation for ritual, handheld pursuit for panic, slow push-in for realization, overhead distance for isolation, or a widening pullback for consequence. Every camera change must serve the dramatic question.

## Dialogue and narration

Dialogue should expose an objective or relationship, not state the moral. Let a character misunderstand, evade, bargain, boast, or confess. Narration is optional; if used, write it after the silent action is clear and keep it shorter than the visual cause-and-effect it accompanies.

## Final director gate

Before asset generation, the director must be able to state:

- the opening hook in one image;
- the protagonist's repeated behavior;
- the irreversible choice;
- the consequence that proves the theme;
- the final image and its emotional duration;
- why each clip exists.

If any answer is vague, return to the screenplay rather than decorating the prompt.
