# Comedy Legibility and Animated Performance

Use this reference for action comedy, cute-animal leads, anthropomorphic characters, animation/comic drama, and hybrid projects with animated-actor performance inside a photoreal world. It does not require every project to be exaggerated; choose the performance mode in the content profile first.

## Build the episode spine before local gags

A 60–120 second comedy cannot be only a sequence of unrelated mishaps. Write one macro causal chain first:

`inciting incident → protagonist misconception/desire → visible objective → repeated active choices → escalating cost → major reversal → consequence/callback`

Every local gag must do at least one job: increase the protagonist's burden, reveal character, change information, consume time, damage a relationship, close an escape route, or directly trigger the next choice. If removing the gag leaves the macro state unchanged, it is replaceable filler and should be rewritten or removed.

The major reversal must not invent a prize, secret, or rule at the end. Track:

`plant → protagonist_assumption → audience_assumption → escalation → reveal → consequence → callback`

Rules, stakes, and reversal clues must be visible or audible before the protagonist acts. The protagonist may ignore, misread, or overestimate them, but the film cannot hide a rule from the audience and then punish the character with it.

## Let characters experience the inciting incident

Prefer an opportunity, threat, activity, or anomaly encountered in the flow of life: hearing a public announcement while passing, seeing someone else's result, smelling something, receiving a concrete object, or witnessing an event already in progress. Staff exposition may confirm rules, but should not carry the entire opening world model.

Opening checks:

- The audience knows what the characters were doing before the interruption.
- The trigger has a visible or audible source.
- The protagonist makes a first irreversible move.
- By ten seconds, a viewer can say why the character must act now.

## Gag-legibility contract

Write every featured gag as:

`setup → visible_cause → audience_advantage → reaction_hold → payoff → story_consequence`

- **setup:** establish the people, prop count, space, and normal state;
- **visible_cause:** show who did what to cause the result; never spawn the result or repair causality with dialogue;
- **audience_advantage:** state why the audience knows before the character, including sightlines, occlusion, and information gaps;
- **reaction_hold:** preserve roughly 0.6–1.0 seconds after the result for the protagonist, partner, or background reactor;
- **payoff:** confirm the joke with an action, reveal, or short button instead of asking viewers to guess whether the beat was funny;
- **story_consequence:** change the macro state and motivate the next beat.

Sightline logic must hold. If an object lands on a character's head, first prove the character is looking elsewhere and the trajectory stays in a blind spot. If it flies through the character's frontal view, a later search is not credible.

Use a readable side, three-quarter, or overhead view for important trajectories. Reduce camera complexity when the action is already complex. A gag must be understood before it can be funny.

## Background people are a reaction layer, not empty filler

Operating restaurants, streets, events, and public spaces need context-appropriate people, staff, and sound. Select one to three recurring reaction characters as audience proxies and lock their identity, position, and allowed reactions. Other extras maintain natural business without stealing focus.

A useful order is: action lands → 0.2–0.5 seconds later a reactor notices, points, or suppresses laughter → protagonist realizes → partner adds the button. Background laughter cannot precede causality or replace the gag itself.

## Choose a performance mode

Explicitly select one mode:

- **naturalistic:** restrained micro-expression and plausible motion;
- **heightened realism:** real anatomy and photographic materials with amplified timing, pose, ears, tail, eyes, and reaction;
- **animated-actor hybrid:** photoreal world/materials with clear anthropomorphic poses, anticipation, overshoot, settle, reaction holds, and controlled squash-and-stretch;
- **full animation/comic drama:** locked design grammar, expression library, deformation bounds, and motion timing.

Do not automatically reduce “live-action environment plus pet” to ordinary animal behavior. In animated-actor hybrid mode, a locked character may stand upright, put a paw on the hip, think with a paw under the chin, skid to a stop, sneak, freeze in an exaggerated take, or use ears and tail as a strong silhouette.

## Separate animation exaggeration from physics bugs

Animation exaggeration primarily affects character performance: pose, expression, silhouette, timing, temporary compression/stretch, and secondary fur or cloth motion. Props, food, vehicles, furniture, containers, glass, and handoffs still obey continuity and contact physics.

Allowed:

- 0.2–0.4 seconds of anticipation, followed by overshoot and settle;
- readable changes in eyes/pupils, ears, tail, cheeks, and mouth corners;
- temporary 5–12% emotional squash/stretch before returning to locked proportions;
- a caught character freezing while only the eyes or ear tips move;
- upright locomotion or human-like gestures when explicitly allowed by the character bible.

Still reject:

- props, food, clothing, or limbs appearing, duplicating, disappearing, or changing identity;
- bodies penetrating tables, glass, boxes, or other characters;
- teleportation or flight without anticipation, path, and landing;
- using “cartoon” to excuse plate flips, count jumps, wrong direction, or speaker misbinding.

## Build a performance language for every character

The character bible should record:

- `performance_mode` and allowed anthropomorphism;
- body/face/ear/tail expressions for excitement, confidence, guilt, defeat, anger, and tenderness;
- signature anticipation, overshoot, settle, and reaction holds;
- allowed squash/stretch range;
- forbidden poses, ordinary-animal limits, and immutable anatomy;
- amplitude contrast with other characters.

Plan at least three distinct performance states inside each 15–30 second block. A character who keeps the same stance, face, and mouth motion throughout is a performance failure even when dialogue is correct.

## Prompt choreography

Do not stop at “exaggerated, cute, animated.” For each beat, write:

1. pre-performance state and silhouette;
2. 0.2–0.4 seconds of anticipation;
3. primary action plus synchronized face/ears/tail;
4. secondary fur/cloth response;
5. overshoot and settle;
6. 0.6–1.0 second reaction hold;
7. next action or exit state.

Give each beat one primary acting action. Keep the camera readable while the character exaggerates. If impact is needed, place the push-in, foreground wipe, or small camera response at the action landing instead of stacking it onto complex body deformation.

## Acceptance

Watch every featured gag muted and answer:

- What was the normal state?
- Which action caused the result?
- Why did the protagonist not see or understand it?
- On which frame do I know it is the laugh beat?
- Did the reaction and reveal hold long enough?
- How did the gag change the macro story?

Then inspect performance: did the character pass through at least three distinguishable states? Can emotion be read from silhouette and action? Does exaggeration have anticipation, overshoot, and settle? Do real props still preserve count, orientation, and physics? If a core answer is unclear, return to storyboard or prompt design; do not hide it with dialogue, captions, BGM, or canned crowd laughter.
